This section evaluates the system through three complementary benchmarks:
(i)~RL performance on committed datasets, (ii)~ZK proof cost for supported
proof-backed relations and configurations, and (iii)~tamper rejection under adversarial
modifications.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Environments.} We use three environment families:
CartPole-v1 and LunarLander-v3~\cite{brockman2016gym} as self-collected
classic-control benchmarks, and PointMaze from D4RL/Minari~\cite{fu2020d4rl,
minari2023} as a public benchmark source.

\textbf{Datasets.} Six self-collected datasets span two environments at
three data-quality levels each: CartPole and LunarLander, at random, medium and
expert, roughly 50{,}000 transitions apiece and all replay/reward audited. The
medium and expert policies differ only in the skill of the collecting policy;
both collect at $\epsilon=0.02$. Public benchmark datasets are PointMaze-umaze
from Minari at 1k, 10k, 50k and 100k transitions, used for the Merkle-depth
scaling rows; public imports are source-integrity commitments only.

\textbf{Baselines.} For RL performance we evaluate Behavioral Cloning, Offline
DQN, Double DQN and CQL-lite, each over 3 random seeds with 5{,}000 training
steps, under both Adam and SGD. A fifth row, \texttt{double\_dqn\_provable},
runs the configuration the SP1 relation checks: batch size 1, plain SGD at
$0.01$, component-wise gradient clipping, and a target-sync interval of
$2{,}000$.

\textbf{SP1 proving environment.} All proof rows in
\Cref{tab:proof-cost} were generated in a single pass on one AWS g5.2xlarge
instance (NVIDIA A10G, 8 vCPU, 32\,GB) with SP1 6.1.0, so prove times within
the table are comparable. Twelve rows ran on the CUDA prover and thirteen on
the CPU prover; the choice is not ours to make per row, since only the
\texttt{training\_fragment} and \texttt{training\_aggregation} hosts have a
CUDA path. Every row records which prover produced it. Recursive aggregation
runs on GPU only: the CPU prover did not complete within 61\,GB of host memory.

\textbf{Reported numbers.} Tables~\ref{tab:rl-performance}
and~\ref{tab:proof-cost} are generated from the committed CSV files under
\texttt{final\_ndss} and included verbatim; a test fails if the generated
tables drift from the artifacts they are built from.

\textbf{Regression suite.} The full Python regression runs 15 checks with 0
failures (\texttt{artifacts/regression\_summary.json}).

\textbf{Analysis.} Four patterns emerge, and one exception.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Verification is fast and flat.} Twenty-four of the twenty-five
  proof-verified rows verify in 0.054--0.197\,s, and the figure does not track
  the work proved: a recursive proof over $1.68$ billion cycles verifies in
  $0.054$\,s, faster than a Merkle membership proof over $117$ thousand cycles
  at $0.123$\,s. Verification cost is set by the proof system, not by the
  computation.

  \item \textbf{Proof sizes are compact, with one exception.} The same
  twenty-four rows range from $1.27$\,MB to $4.31$\,MB. Recursive proofs are
  the \emph{smallest} at $1.27$\,MB despite covering the most work, which is
  the compression that makes recursion worth its prover cost.

  \item \textbf{The Groth16 variant is the exception on both axes.} Verifying
  Groth16 child proofs inside the guest costs $6.18$ billion cycles, a
  $1.4$\,GB proof, and $68.0$\,s to verify -- three orders of magnitude above
  every other row on size and verify time. We report it because it is the one
  configuration where in-guest verification of a SNARK, rather than of a native
  SP1 proof, was measured; it is not a configuration we would recommend.

  \item \textbf{Recursion cost is per child, not per step.} Flat recursive
  aggregation costs $422.7$M, $842.0$M and $1683.5$M cycles at $T = 16, 32, 64$,
  which is $211$M, $211$M and $211$M cycles per child proof verified. The cost
  does not depend on how many training steps each child covers, so a chain of
  longer leaves is cheaper for the same trace length. This is why the whole-run
  proofs below use $156$-step leaves rather than $8$-step leaves.

  \item \textbf{Merkle cost grows with depth, not size.} Dataset scaling from
  $1$k to $100$k leaves moves cycles from $358$k to $554$k -- a $100\times$
  increase in dataset size for a $1.5\times$ increase in cycles, consistent
  with a per-level cost over a tree of depth $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$.
\end{itemize}

Cycle counts are nearly identical across the three because the root node
verifies two child proofs in every case; the tree depth below it, and hence the
trace length, does not enter the root's own cost. The $4{,}992$-step row is the
configuration Table~\ref{tab:rl-performance} reports at $-152.7$ on
\texttt{lunarlander-random}, so the proof covers the run that produced a number
in the table rather than a run alongside it.

Two limits bound the trace length, and neither is the proof system. First,
arithmetic: offline DQN diverges, and at a target-sync interval of $4$ the
fixed-point $Q$ values crossed the declared bound before step $1{,}404$. At the
interval of $2{,}000$ the reported configuration uses, a simulation of all
$32$ chunks put peak $|Q|$ at $1.01\times 10^{5}$ against a declared bound of
$4.50\times 10^{15}$, so the wall moved by nine orders of magnitude. Second,
cost: a CartPole run long enough to learn well needs roughly $50{,}000$ steps,
which at this leaf size is $321$ leaves, and we estimate $68$ GPU-hours from
the measured per-leaf time. We did not run it.

\subsection{What the Provable Configuration Costs}
\label{subsec:provable-config}

The relation checks plain SGD on single transitions with component-wise
clipping, which is not what a tuned implementation runs. We measure that gap
rather than leave it to the reader. \texttt{double\_dqn\_provable} in
\Cref{tab:rl-performance} runs exactly the relation's settings; on
\texttt{lunarlander-random} it reaches $-152.7$ against $-215.3$ for the tuned
minibatch row, and it improves on its own previous configuration on all six
datasets.

Which of the three differences accounts for the gap is not obvious, so we
changed one at a time. On \texttt{cartpole-random} at $5{,}000$ steps, one
seed: the tuned configuration scores $78.0$; substituting component-wise for
$L_2$ clipping gives $75.4$; taking the target-sync interval from $100$ to $4$
gives $9.4$; taking the batch size from $256$ to $1$ gives $9.4$. The
substitution the proof forces costs about $3\%$, and the two settings that
collapse the score are the sync interval and the minibatch.

That result is easy to over-read, and we did. Each of those two was varied with
the other held at its tuned value, which licenses no conclusion about their
combination. A sweep of $60$ cells at batch size 1 shows batch size 1 fails at
a sync interval of $100$ and succeeds at $2{,}000$: on \texttt{cartpole-random}
it moves from $9.6$ to $209.5$, and on \texttt{lunarlander-random} from
$-649.1$ to $-197.4$. The setting that mattered was the one already exposed as
a free public input; the one that would have cost cycles per step was not the
obstacle. We report the first ablation and its correction because the
correction is the more useful result.

\subsection{A Defect the Tamper Suite Could Not See}
\label{subsec:sampling-defect}

During development the sampler used a fixed seed with a step index local to
each fragment. Every chunk of a chain therefore drew the same indices: chunk
$0$ (steps $0$--$7$) and chunk $1$ (steps $8$--$15$) both selected
$[68, 83, 22, 125, 56, 55, 42, 1]$. A $1{,}248$-step chain was $156$
repetitions over eight rows of a $50{,}000$-row dataset rather than a pass over
the data.

Nothing caught it. Every hash agreed, every proof verified, and all $236$
tamper cases stayed green, because no field had been altered -- the relation
computed exactly what it said it computed, and what it said was not what we
believed. \Cref{tab:tamper-summary} tests whether a modified artifact is
rejected; it cannot test whether an unmodified relation means what its authors
intended.

We record this for two reasons. It is the sharpest evidence we have that
sampling integrity is a real verification obligation rather than a theoretical
one, and it bounds what a field-level tamper benchmark establishes. Binding the
sampler to the dataset commitment fixes the defect and removes the prover's
freedom to grind the seed in the same change.

\textbf{Analysis.} The tamper-rejection results demonstrate several important
properties:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{No false negatives.} Every adversarial modification across all
  19 categories is detected and rejected. There are zero ``accepted
  unexpectedly'' rows.

  \item \textbf{Multi-layer defense.} Tampering is rejected at different
  layers depending on the component and modification type: dataset audit,
  dataset commitment verification, Python semantic oracle, Rust execute mode,
  SP1 proof verification, or public-input binding checks.

  \item \textbf{Comprehensive coverage.} The tamper suite covers
  data-level attacks (reward, next state, done, action), commitment-level
  attacks (Merkle path, leaf, root), computation-level attacks (Q value, TD
  target, gradient, loss), trace-level attacks (checkpoint hash, target sync),
  and aggregation-level attacks (proof public input, manifest hash).
\end{itemize}
